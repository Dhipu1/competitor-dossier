Why most competitor audits don't survive contact with a client

The usual version is a person clicking through four rival websites for a day and writing up what stuck. It reads fine. It's also unrepeatable, skewed by whichever pages happened to get opened, and impossible to check — when a client asks "where did this come from?", the honest answer is "I remember seeing it somewhere on their blog."

This runs as a pipeline instead. The same sites, the same method, every time — and every finding carries the URL it was drawn from.

Measured, not guessed

Every passage a competitor publishes is compared against the closest equivalent anywhere on your site. The weakest matches are the gaps. That's a number, not an opinion.

Sourced to the page

Each finding cites the exact competitor URL behind it. You can open it and check. Nothing is written from memory or general knowledge about your market.

Checked before it ships

Every citation is verified against the crawl automatically. A report referencing a page that was never fetched doesn't get delivered — it fails the build.

Method

Seven stages, run in order, every time

Nothing here is improvised per client. The same pipeline runs for an audit and for a monitoring check — monitoring simply adds a comparison against the previous run and reports only what moved.

01

Crawl

Your site and each named competitor, fetched with a real browser so JavaScript-rendered pages are captured as a visitor would see them. robots.txt and rate limits respected on every request.

02

Extract

Navigation, cookie banners, and footers stripped away, leaving the actual content of each page. A typical page reduces from 400KB of markup to the few thousand words that matter.

03

Enrich

On-page signals read from the markup — title and description coverage, heading structure, content depth, internal linking, structured data — plus each site's own sitemap for scale and publishing cadence.

04

Segment

Pages split into passages at their own heading boundaries, so a comparison is made against a coherent section rather than an arbitrary slice of text.

05

Compare

Every passage converted into a numerical representation of its meaning, so "release notes" and "patch log" register as the same subject even though they share no words.

06

Measure

For each competitor passage, the closest thing on your site is found and scored. A distant closest match means they cover ground you don't — ranked by how large that distance is.

07

Report

Findings written up with a citation on every claim, then each citation checked back against the crawl. Delivered as a PDF with a walkthrough call.

What isn't measured, and why we say so

Plenty of audits quietly present estimates as findings. These are the limits of this one, stated up front rather than discovered later:

  • No search volumes, rankings, or traffic figures. Those can't be derived from a page — they need a paid data subscription, quoted separately if you want them. No number in a report is a ranking.
  • Sites publishing no sitemap are reported as unknown scale. Not as small, not as inactive. Unknown means unknown.
  • Page samples are samples. A crawl depth is agreed before we start, and the report says what it was rather than implying the whole site was read.
  • Pages behind a login aren't touched, nor is anything a site's robots.txt asks crawlers to leave alone.

Questions

Asked before, answered honestly

Do you scrape Google for our rankings?

No. Scraping search results is against Google's terms, technically fragile, and gets IPs banned mid-project. Ranking and search-volume data comes from a licensed provider, quoted separately, or it isn't in the report at all. Nothing gets estimated and presented as measured.

How do I know the report isn't just plausible-sounding AI output?

Because the findings aren't written by a model — they're measured, then explained by one. The gap list comes out of scoring every competitor passage against your site's closest equivalent. The write-up is only allowed to discuss findings that already exist in that data, and every cited URL is checked back against the crawl before delivery. A report citing a page we never fetched fails the check and isn't sent.

Is crawling our competitors' sites legal?

We fetch public pages, at a polite rate, obeying each site's robots.txt — the same thing every search engine does. Nothing behind a login is touched, and no site is hit hard enough to be a nuisance. Crawl scope is agreed with you in writing before anything starts.

What if a competitor's site has almost no content?

Then that's the finding, and it's stated as one. The sample audit found one competitor averaging 74 words a page against the client's 652 — the client was comfortably ahead, and the report said so first.

Why do you need Search Console access?

You don't have to grant it, but it's the single biggest free upgrade available. Search Console is Google's own first-party record of your site: real queries, real impressions, real positions. Read-only access takes a minute to grant and no purchased dataset is more accurate for your own pages. It only ever covers sites you own, so competitor analysis stays with what we crawl.

What does the monitoring retainer actually send me?

A short brief on what changed since the last check, competitor activity first. Unchanged pages are filtered out before anything is written, so you're not reading a monthly restatement of things you already know. If nothing meaningful happened, the brief says that in a line rather than padding to look busy.

Can I have the raw data?

Yes. The crawled pages, the extracted text, the scores behind every finding — all of it is yours on request. There's nothing in the method that only works if you can't see inside it.
